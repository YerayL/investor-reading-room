Lightspeed’s Ravi Mhatre & Bejul Somaia on the AI bubble, India opportunity & more

Ravi Mhatre · 2025-11-19 · 书面问答或编辑访谈 · The Economic Times

出版方原文

Menlo Park: Lightspeed Venture Partners, one of the most prominent early-stage investors in Silicon Valley, says the euphoria around artificial intelligence valuations does not change the fact that this is a once-in-a-generation transformation and that it'll rewire all sectors and businesses going forward. Lightspeed, which manages more than $28 billion in assets globally, has backed AI companies including Anthropic, where it led a $3.5 billion round earlier this year, Elon Musk's XAI, France's Mistral and Indian startup Sarvam AI, in its big push across both the foundation and application layer of the technology. Its cofounder and partner Ravi Mhatre, along with partner Bejul Somaia who moved to the San Francisco Bay Area after leading the VC firm's India franchise, spoke about the AI bubble, India's opportunities in the sector and the firm's India performance in an exclusive interaction with ET at Lightspeed's Menlo Park headquarters. Edited excerpts:

How do you look at India as a market for AI investments?

Mhatre: India is a massive usage market, one of the largest globally for any digital platform. Second, it's a talent-density market for AI. India, because of its deep IT ecosystem and Indo-US tech corridor, has a concentration of such talent. That's one reason why all major AI labs are now present there. We're seeing the beginnings of more AI-native companies emerge from India, particularly at application layer.

Tying that to Anthropic, where does India fit in? Is it largely a user market for large firms?

Mhatre: India is already Anthropic's second-largest market globally in terms of usage. It mirrors what we've seen historically with platforms like Google, LinkedIn, or Instagram-massive adoption in India right behind the US. But beyond usage, India plays a deeper role. A lot of developers here are building AI-powered products. India has long been a hub for technology-assisted labour, from BPO to IT services. AI is now transforming that into 'AI-assisted labour', where repetitive tasks are automated but humans design and manage the workflows. That's a natural extension of India's outsourcing story.

How do you justify these valuations as someone betting on AI?

Mhatre: AI's evolution is exponential, not linear. Each leap in capability multiplies impact rather than adding incrementally. Historically, such shifts, from the internet to smartphones, have redefined industries. With AI, these leaps are happening faster. So, while valuations are high, the potential value creation over the next decade could surpass previous tech cycles.

Somaia: Hype cycles blur risk perception. Every firm with early traction cannot command billion-dollar valuations. Most of our capital goes early, and we double down selectively on clear winners.

There's a sense that a lot of AI partnerships like those of OpenAI or Anthropic involve circular deals, making revenue quality questionable. Where are you on the talk about an AI bubble?

Mhatre: Yes, we are in a bubble, but that does not make the transformation any less real. AI is a once-in-a-generation shift, and the capital chasing it reflects that. Both can be true: hype exists, but so do enduring opportunities. The key is to identify which technologies will sustain once the noise fades. Many speculative models will collapse, but those solving real problems will endure.

What about the application layer, where do you see India here?

Somaia: We look at the AI stack in three layers: foundation models, enablement and tooling (that make models consumable) and applications built on top. Most of India's opportunities will be in the second and third layers. For example, we've invested in companies in healthcare revenue cycle management, AI-enabled accounting, and automation verticals that already export services to the US. These businesses can now layer AI on top of existing labour-based workflows to drive efficiency and value. On consumer side, India's advantage is the absence of Big Tech dominance in several verticals, creating space for local AI-native consumer products to emerge.

Is it hard being an early-stage investor in the Valley, especially because the big players are dominating this cycle?

Somaia: Over the next decade, entire sectors will be rewired by AI. We are already seeing it in verticals like law and healthcare, from patent research to medical billing. As the cost of intelligence falls, these niches expand quickly. We have backed Skild AI in robotics and companies like Glean and Abridge in healthcare. Not every startup will be a unicorn, but even smaller outcomes can be meaningful if you enter early.

The Indian government has been talking about building ‘sovereign AI’. How do you see India’s position in building local foundation models given the capital intensity and global competition?

Mhatre: Sovereign AI is a meaningful trend globally. We’re investors in Mistral, a European foundation-model company born from similar motivations. Many countries now see native AI capabilities as essential for both national security and linguistic accessibility. In India’s case, with its plurality of languages, it’s especially critical. That’s why we’ve backed Sarvam AI, which is building foundation models in India.

Somaia: When we invested in Sarvam, part of the thesis was that the founders had experience building population-scale systems. At the same time, we have to recognise the market reality—users and enterprises will still adopt the best model for their needs. Unless governments create strict barriers, AI will remain a global, competitive market. So, we’ll see both: sovereign models for specific local or regulated use cases, and global models dominating broader applications.

What about the infrastructure side, data centres? Will that be a big part of India’s AI ecosystem?

Somaia: Given India’s scale of AI usage, local infrastructure investment is inevitable. But there are challenges, mainly around the reliability and cost of power. Certain states will likely benefit more, depending on how efficiently they address that. Over the next decade, we’ll definitely see significant investment in AI infrastructure here.

How’s Lightspeed doing overall in India? There were concerns around Sharechat, Udaan…and some others portfolio firms...

Somaia: I’ve learned over time that things are rarely as good or as bad as they seem. The core question is always whether the companies we’ve backed are creating real value and many are doing just that. After the volatility of the Covid period, most of our portfolio is on solid footing, with management teams focused on building durable businesses.

There’s been chatter about Lightspeed’s India performance and the internal transition after you moved to the US…

Somaia: I don’t spend much time on perception. The India & Southeast Asia team continues to perform well with distributed performance across the partnership, strong portfolio company progression and an IPO around the corner. We’ve always been a close-knit, collaborative partnership, and that remains true today.

But did your move to the US impact the team in India?

Somaia: Any transition brings change, but we’ve worked through it together. The team today is in a very strong place. And this transition is part of a larger evolution across Lightspeed globally, we’re becoming even more collaborative as a worldwide partnership.

You’re planning to raise a new India fund next year which is expected to be far smaller in size–around $275-300 million– compared with the $500 million closed in 2022…

Somaia: We can’t discuss fundraising. But the broader market has shifted since the exuberance of a few years ago, and our approach, as always, will be measured and consistent with the opportunity ahead.

Synopsis

Lightspeed Venture Partners, a leading Silicon Valley-based investment firm, is of the view that despite the current valuation "euphoria" in artificial intelligence (AI), the technology represents a once-in-a-generation transformation that will rewire all sectors and businesses.
